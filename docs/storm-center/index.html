What to Do Immediately After the Storm

Once it is safe to do so, check for immediate hazards such as downed power lines, gas leaks, standing water near electrical sources, or structural damage before entering the property.

Take reasonable steps to prevent additional damage if you can do so safely. Depending on the circumstances, this may include temporary roof protection, water extraction, or other emergency measures. Photograph the condition of the property and keep contracts, invoices, and receipts for any work performed.

Photograph and video the damage before cleanup or permanent repairs begin whenever possible. Report the claim to your insurer promptly. Florida law generally requires notice of a new or reopened property insurance claim within one year after the date of loss and notice of a supplemental claim within 18 months.

Be cautious with contractors who arrive in the neighborhood immediately after a storm. Verify licenses where applicable, get the scope and price of the work in writing, and read anything you are asked to sign before signing it.

Before Landfall: A Short Checklist

1Document the property before the storm. Take photographs or video of the rooms inside your home, the roof and exterior where safely accessible, outdoor structures, and significant personal property.
2Locate your complete insurance policy. Keep a copy somewhere you can access if your home loses power, internet service, or becomes inaccessible.
3Check your hurricane deductible. Review your declarations page so you know the deductible that may apply and your dwelling coverage limit.
4Protect the property if it is safe to do so. Secure loose outdoor items and install shutters or other approved protection if you have them and conditions allow.
5Save important contact information. Keep your insurer’s claim-reporting information, emergency contacts, and this page somewhere you can access after the storm.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do I have to file a hurricane damage claim in Florida?

Florida law generally requires notice of a new or reopened property insurance claim within one year after the date of loss. A supplemental claim generally must be reported within 18 months after the date of loss.

Waiting can create additional problems even when the statutory deadline has not expired. Report known damage as soon as reasonably possible and keep records of when and how you reported it.

Can I supplement my claim if additional damage or repair costs are discovered later?

Potentially, yes. Florida law recognizes supplemental property insurance claims, but they are subject to an 18-month notice deadline and the terms and conditions of the policy. Keep documentation of additional damage and repair costs as they become known.

How does my hurricane deductible work?

Florida personal-lines residential policies commonly have a separate hurricane deductible. Depending on the policy, it may be stated as a percentage of the dwelling limit or another permitted amount.

For personal-lines residential policies subject to Florida’s hurricane-deductible statute, the hurricane deductible generally applies on an annual basis to covered hurricane losses occurring during the same calendar year. Your declarations page and policy will identify the deductible that applies to your property.

What if I cannot stay in my home after the storm?

Many homeowners policies provide additional living expense coverage when a covered loss makes the home uninhabitable. Depending on the policy, this may include certain temporary housing and related additional expenses.

Keep receipts and records of what you spend. Coverage and applicable limits depend on your policy and the circumstances of the loss.

Do I have to use the insurance company’s preferred contractor?

Not necessarily. Many policyholders can select their own contractor, but some policies contain managed-repair, preferred-contractor, or election-to-repair provisions that can affect how repairs are handled.

Before agreeing to a repair program or signing a contract, review the applicable policy language and the documents the insurer has provided.

My insurer says the roof damage is wear and tear, not storm damage. What now?

Disputes over whether roof damage resulted from a storm, age, deterioration, installation issues, or another cause are common after hurricanes and severe weather.

Photographs, inspection findings, repair history, weather information, and other evidence can be important in evaluating the carrier’s conclusion. We can review the denial or coverage letter and the evidence supporting the claim to determine what the carrier relied on and what may have been overlooked.

Is flood damage covered under my homeowners policy?

Standard homeowners policies generally exclude flooding caused by rising surface water. Flood coverage is commonly purchased separately through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood insurer.

Wind damage may be covered under a homeowners policy. Coverage for rain or other water entering the property depends on the policy language, the cause of the opening or intrusion, and the facts of the loss.

What happens if my insurance company becomes insolvent after a major storm?

If a Florida property insurer is placed into liquidation, the Florida Insurance Guaranty Association may become responsible for certain eligible covered claims, subject to statutory requirements, exclusions, limits, and deadlines.

An insolvency can also affect the status of the existing insurance policy. Policyholders should pay close attention to notices concerning cancellation dates and replacement coverage.

When should I contact an attorney about my hurricane claim?

You do not have to wait until the insurance company denies the claim.

We review claims at different stages of the process, including before an inspection, while the carrier is investigating, after a request for additional information, and after a claim has been denied, delayed, or underpaid. An early review can help identify what the policy requires, what documentation may still be needed, and whether there are problems with the way the claim is being handled.
